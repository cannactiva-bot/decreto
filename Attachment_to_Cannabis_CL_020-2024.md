# The Implementation of the Provisions of the 1961 Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs as Amended by the 1972 Protocol, and the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances in Relation to Cannabis and Cannabis-Related Substances

## Limitations on the Use of Cannabis and Cannabis-Related Substances

1. Article 4(c) of the 1961 Convention, as amended, includes the following general obligation: *"Subject to the provisions of this Convention, the production, manufacture, export, import, distribution, trade, use, and possession of drugs shall be limited exclusively to medical and scientific purposes."*

2. Similarly, article 5 and article 7 of the 1971 Convention limits the manufacture, export, import, distribution, trade, use, and possession of psychotropic substances to medical and scientific purposes.

## Definitions

### Article 1, Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs

> **(b)** "Cannabis" means the flowering or fruiting tops of the cannabis plant (excluding the seeds and leaves when not accompanied by the tops) from which the resin has not been extracted, by whatever name they may be designated.
>
> **(c)** "Cannabis plant" means any plant of the genus Cannabis.
>
> **(d)** "Cannabis resin" means the separated resin, whether crude or purified, obtained from the cannabis plant.

3. The definitions of cannabis and the cannabis plant are provided in Article 1 of the 1961 Convention, and both are subject to international control under the same Convention. The flowering and fruiting tops of cannabis plants are controlled under the 1961 Convention, as amended by the 1972 Protocol, regardless of whether they are dried or not.

4. THC, its isomers, and delta-9-THC are the only cannabinoids controlled under the 1971 Convention, listed in Schedule I and Schedule II, respectively. CBD is not included in the Convention's schedules and is therefore not subject to control.

5. The 1961 Convention authorizes the cultivation of cannabis for medical and scientific purposes, regardless of cannabinoid type, levels, or specific extraction purposes. Only cannabis cultivation for industrial purposes (fibre and seed) or horticulture is exempt from international control. Governments must establish a control system to ensure adherence to the Convention's provisions.

6. The 1961 Convention allows States to establish programs for providing cannabis for medical purposes. Recognizing the risk of diversion into illicit channels and misuse, where such programs do not implement adequate and effective control measures, the Conventions subject the establishment and operation of medical cannabis use to a set of strict requirements.

7. In monitoring State compliance with the drug control conventions, INCB has noted that control measures in many existing programs do not always meet the minimum requirements set forth in the Conventions.

### Article 1, Convention on Psychotropic Substances

> **(e)** "Psychotropic substance" means any substance, natural or synthetic, or any natural material in Schedule I, II, III or IV.
>
> **(g)** "Schedule I", "Schedule II", "Schedule III" and "Schedule IV" mean the correspondingly numbered lists of psychotropic substances annexed to this Convention, as altered in accordance with article 2.

8. Article 7 of the 1971 Convention includes special provisions for the use of psychotropic substances listed in Schedule I, including THC and its isomers, with the exception of delta-9-THC. These provisions prohibit all uses of Schedule I substances except for scientific and very limited medical purposes. Additionally, Article 7 requires special licenses for any authorized use of these substances, as well as for international trade.

## Reporting

9. After CND considered the WHO recommendations on cannabis and cannabis-related substances in December 2020, INCB held various consultations with Member States and experts to revise the reporting requirements to achieve harmonization. As a result of these consultations, INCB introduced new reporting requirements, effective as of 2024.

10. Cannabis and cannabis resin must be reported in accordance with the forms related to the 1961 Convention. In addition to cannabis and cannabis resin, extracts and tinctures of cannabis are listed in Schedule I of the 1961 Convention. However, INCB does not recommend reporting extracts and tinctures due to the lack of a clear definition in the Convention.

11. INCB recommends reporting any preparation or by-product derived from cannabis in terms of the cannabinoids it contains under the 1971 Convention. The Conventions do not establish a threshold for the amount of THC in order for a preparation derived from cannabis to be controlled.

12. Reporting to INCB annually, by 30 June, on estimates of cannabis and cannabis resin requirements through Form B, and supplementary estimates of cannabis and cannabis resin requirements through the Supplement to Form B (in accordance with Articles 1, 12, and 19 of the Single Convention).

13. Reporting to INCB on annual assessments of delta-9-THC¹ requirements through Form B/P should be at the interval of every three years at a minimum, and supplementary estimates of delta-9-THC requirements through the Supplement to Form B/P at any time (pursuant to ECOSOC resolutions 1981/7, 1991/44, 1993/38, and 1996/30). Assessments are not required for THC since it is included in Schedule I of the 1971 Convention.

14. Reporting to INCB should be annually, by 30 June, on statistics of production, manufacture, consumption, stocks, and seizures of cannabis and cannabis resin, including data on the area of cultivation and geographical location of the cannabis plant, through Form C (in accordance with Articles 1, 2, 13, and 20 of the Single Convention).

15. Reporting to INCB should also be annually, by 30 June, on statistics related to the manufacture, import, export, utilization, consumption, and manufacturers' stocks of THC and delta-9-THC², through Form P (as required under the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances: Articles 1, 2, 3, 12, and 16; Resolution I of the United Nations Conference for the Adoption of a Protocol on Psychotropic Substances; and ECOSOC resolutions 1576 (L), 1985/15, 1987/30, and CND resolution 54/6).

16. Reporting to INCB is on a quarterly basis, no later than one month after the end of the relevant quarter, on statistics of imports and exports of narcotic drugs through Form A (in accordance with Articles 1, 2, 13, and 20 of the Single Convention).

17. Reporting to INCB is also on a quarterly basis, no later than one month after the end of the relevant quarter, on statistics of imports and exports of delta-9-THC³ through Form A/P (pursuant to ECOSOC resolutions 1981/7, 1991/44, 1993/38, and 1996/30).

## Agency

### Article 28, Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs

> "If a Party permits the cultivation of the cannabis plant for the production of cannabis or cannabis resin, it shall apply thereto the system of controls as provided in article 23 respecting the control of the opium poppy."

### Article 23, Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs

> "A Party that permits the cultivation of the opium poppy for the production of opium shall establish, if it has not already done so, and maintain, one or more government agencies (hereafter in this article referred to as the Agency) to carry out the functions required under this article."
>
> "The Agency shall designate the areas in which, and the plots of land on which, cultivation of the opium poppy for the purpose of producing opium shall be permitted."
>
> "Only cultivators licensed by the Agency shall be authorized to engage in such cultivation. Each licence shall specify the extent of the land on which the cultivation is permitted."

18. The treaties outline requirements for States parties on how they may permit the use of cannabis and its derivatives for medical purposes. For example, Articles 23 and 28 of the 1961 Convention, as amended, require governments to establish a national cannabis agency to control production and regulate the supply of cannabinoids for medical use. The national agency is responsible for licensing producers, purchasing and managing stock, and maintaining a monopoly on wholesale trading and stockpiling. The agency must also provide annual estimates to INCB on the quantities of cannabis to be used for medical purposes, as well as the estimated number of patients who will be treated with the drug. This does not necessarily require the creation of a new institution; these functions can be assigned to an existing government agency with the capacity to perform them.

### Physical Possession and Exclusive Rights

#### Article 23, Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs

> "All cultivators of the opium poppy shall be required to deliver their total crops of opium to the Agency. The Agency shall purchase and take physical possession of such crops as soon as possible, but not later than four months after the end of the harvest."
>
> "The Agency shall, in respect of opium, have the exclusive right of importing, exporting, wholesale trading and maintaining stocks other than those held by manufacturers of opium alkaloids, medicinal opium or opium preparations. Parties need not extend this exclusive right to medicinal opium and opium preparations."

19. The control requirements for the opium poppy apply *mutatis mutandis* to medical cannabis, including the requirement for the agency to take physical possession of stocks. These requirements, defined in 1961 primarily in relation to opium control as a crop harvested from the opium poppy, arose due to the high risk of opium diversion. As a result, the drafters of the Convention required government agencies to take physical possession of the crops. However, the situation with cannabis is different. INCB, recognizing that many functions under the Convention are now carried out by private actors operating under government licenses rather than by state agencies, has decided to waive this requirement, provided the state maintains active and effective oversight.

20. In accordance with Articles 2, 22, and 31 of the Single Convention a Party prohibiting the cultivation of the cannabis plant shall take appropriate measures to seize and destroy illicit crops whenever deemed necessary to protect public health and prevent illicit trafficking.

21. Also, in accordance with Article 28 of the Single Convention parties shall adopt necessary measures to prevent the misuse of, and illicit trafficking in cannabis leaves.

## Self-Cultivation for Personal Medical Purposes

22. Cultivation, including self-cultivation, for medical purposes must meet the requirements of the Single Convention set forth in article 28 and article 23. Countries, while regulating the medical use of cannabis for patients, should prevent diversion. Allowing private individuals to produce cannabis for personal medical use presents health risks, as dosages and THC levels consumed may vary from those medically prescribed.

## Cultivation for Industrial Purposes and Exemption for "Hemp"

### Article 28, Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs

> "This Convention shall not apply to the cultivation of the cannabis plant exclusively for industrial purposes (fibre and seed) or horticultural purposes."

23. The system of control of cannabis set out in article 28, as provided in article 23, of the Single Convention does not apply to the cultivation of cannabis plant exclusively for industrial purposes (fibre and seed) or horticultural purposes. However, the specification of fibre and seed in the Single Convention can be seen as an example. Therefore, the cultivation of cannabis plant with low THC content (commonly referred to as "hemp") for the extraction of cannabinoids that are not internationally controlled can be considered as industrial cultivation. Countries are required to ensure that any internationally controlled cannabinoids obtained as a byproduct of the extraction process need to be controlled and reported in accordance with the requirements of the conventions.
